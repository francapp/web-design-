Overview

Body Health manufactures professional aesthetic devices. For their new line the goal wasn't to paint over the existing screen: the enclosure, applicators, and operating system had to be rethought as a single product, capable of supporting four different therapies — CryoFrequency, HImFU, Enygma, and ReFreeze — without each one behaving like a separate device.

I joined as Product Designer Sr., responsible for research and product decisions: which variables users needed to control, how the device could be learned without a manual, what had to be preset vs. manual, and how one system could serve both an aesthetic physician and a cosmetologist who rents the device by season. The UI was the result of those definitions, not the starting point.

Empathize

I started by listening to those already using the device. First a survey with current clients to understand what they valued and what they would change, then in-depth interviews with users and potential buyers. What surfaced wasn't an aesthetic problem — it was a comprehension problem.

Interface preferences completed the picture: 70% wanted more color on screen, and on ideal size 62% chose 14" vs. 26% who preferred 10" and 12% who wanted 20". On what to control during a session, 48% wanted power, time, and body zone; 42% wanted time and session; only 10% were satisfied with time alone. And in treatment configuration the core tension of the project emerged: 50% wanted therapy-specific presets, 38% wanted to control as many variables as possible, and only 12% were content with the current system.

With the material on the table I grouped findings into concrete pain points and turned them into decision criteria for the rest of the project.

From that I defined the product goals: simplify technical language and allow the manual to be consulted from the device itself, reduce setup time, build a system that works for both the expert operator — with manual control — and the new one, with presets; add internet connectivity for remote updates; and leave an interface scalable across the entire line, with room for future treatments.

Ideate

Before drawing any screens I focused on understanding what users expected the device to do. Most came from working with the previous line: they already had a mental model, internalized gestures, and a way of naming things. Breaking that completely would have forced them to relearn the device; keeping it as-is would have carried the same problems forward.

The direction was to preserve what they already knew and address the pain points one by one: simpler language, clear hierarchy, recognizable patterns, and in-system support. In parallel I worked on the moodboard to define what we wanted the device to convey: warmth, precision, and friendly technology — closer to a consumer object — speakers, thermostats, wearables — than a medical device. That intention later guided the palette, materials, and interface treatment.

Moodboard: the desired perception for the device — warm, precise, and familiar.

Grids for two screens

With the flow defined I brought the structure down to the two formats that had to be solved simultaneously: the main device, with a 14" 16:9 tablet, and the "child" device, with a 12" 16:10 tablet. I adapted the grid between both so components were exactly the same and only the spacing changed — never the hierarchy or the flow. That decision is what later allowed the entire line to share one operating system.

Six columns in both formats: 375 px column and 40 px gutter at 1920×1080, 158 px and 25 px at 1280×800.

The design system

I built the component system that underpins all four therapies: start and stop treatment states, session controls — timer, depth, power, and temperature —, body diagrams, typography, scale, and the animations for each interaction. All designed so that adding a new therapy means assembling existing pieces, not redesigning screens.

Components, states, and session controls.

Test

I validated the system with 20 usability tests across the 32 designed screens, in both screen sizes. The focus was on the tasks research had flagged as critical: configuring a treatment without opening the manual, understanding the hierarchy of the session screen, and switching between preset mode and manual control without losing track.

Outcome

The Unyque line now has a single, consistent operating system: all four therapies share structure, components, and vocabulary, and the device can add future treatments without redesigning from scratch. The same interface works on both 12" and 14" screens, and expert mode and presets — what half the clients had asked for — coexist smoothly.

The project — enclosures, applicators, and interface — received Argentina's Good Design Seal.

Takeaways

The most uncomfortable finding from the research was the most useful: half the clients wanted presets and nearly four in ten wanted full control. Resolving that as a product decision — two modes coexisting in a single flow — instead of averaging preferences was what made the system scalable.

I also confirmed that in hardware, design doesn't end at the screen: working on the software alongside the enclosure and applicators, and sitting down with the medical team from the paper sketch stage, prevented interface decisions that would have had no physical support later on.
